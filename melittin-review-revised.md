# Engineering selectivity into melittin, a nonselective peptide, as a cancer therapeutic

Sheridan Metzger · Oct 5, 2026

## Abstract

Melittin, the principal peptide of honeybee venom, lyses cells by physically disrupting the phospholipid bilayer. This mechanism is indifferent to metabolic state, efflux capacity, and apoptotic blockade, and it is equally indifferent to whether the target is malignant. Native melittin damages erythrocytes at concentrations below those required to kill tumor cells, a selectivity index under unity that has blocked systemic use. This review examines three strategies for resolving that barrier: modification of the peptide sequence, direction toward tumor-associated receptors, and encapsulation in nanocarriers. The question asked of each is what it charges in potency, and the exchange rate varies widely. Substitution at tryptophan 19 reduced hemolysis roughly thirteenfold against a threefold loss in anticancer activity, inverting the selectivity index. Conditional activation and receptor targeting avoid the exchange by killing through a route that does not require lysis at all. Nanocarriers reduce exposure to circulating blood cells, though protection alone confers no selectivity. The most favorable results share a mechanism: they exploit an asymmetry in how cancer cells and healthy cells can be killed, preserving apoptotic or receptor-mediated activity while forfeiting lysis. Evidence remains preclinical, hemolysis remains dose-limiting, and delivery is unresolved. Combining conditional activation with sub-lytic receptor targeting, approaches that have not yet been tested together, is the most promising untested direction.

## Introduction

Melittin kills nearly anything with a phospholipid membrane, which is both the reason it works as an anticancer agent and the reason it cannot be given systemically. The principal peptide component of honeybee (*Apis mellifera*) venom, melittin has shown cytotoxicity across a wide range of preclinical cancer models, and a substantial body of cell culture and animal work supports its activity as a regulator of cancer cell proliferation.<sup>1</sup> None of that work has resolved the problem that defines the field. Melittin recognizes a bilayer, not a tumor.

The structural source of this behavior has been characterized in detail. Ramirez et al. solved the solution structure of recombinant melittin by NMR and found a long helical molecule in which N-terminal and C-terminal α-helices are joined by a noncanonical 3₁₀-helix in the middle.<sup>2</sup> That central segment is absent from the earlier X-ray structure of tetrameric melittin and from the NMR structure determined in methanol, so the monomer adopts a conformation the tetramer does not. The amphipathic organization this creates, with hydrophobic and charged faces separated along the helix, is what allows the peptide to partition into a bilayer and disrupt it. It is also the structure that any attempt at engineering selectivity has to modify.

The clearest demonstration of what melittin can do comes from outside oncology. Socarras et al. tested whole bee venom and purified melittin against *Borrelia burgdorferi*, the spirochete responsible for Lyme disease, using viability dyes, direct cell counts, biofilm assays, and atomic force microscopy.<sup>3</sup> Melittin disrupted the bacterial cell membrane, causing leakage of cell contents and loss of the metabolic function required for survival. What distinguishes the result is the comparison the authors drew. *B. burgdorferi* persists not only as a spirochete but as round bodies, stationary-phase persisters, and attached biofilms, and the survival of these alternative forms is thought to drive relapse after standard treatment. Doxycycline, cefoperazone, and daptomycin, antibiotics selected for their reported activity against *Borrelia* persisters, had limited effect against the attached biofilm form whether given individually or in combination. Bee venom and melittin significantly reduced the number and viability of every morphological form tested, biofilms included.

The source of that strength is worth being precise about. Conventional antibiotics fail against persisters and biofilms because those states downregulate the metabolic processes antibiotics depend on. Melittin is indifferent to metabolic state because it does not exploit one, and a dormant cell has a membrane just as an active one does. The same indifference offers no reason to spare a hepatocyte or an erythrocyte. Socarras et al. were explicit on this point, concluding that in vivo evaluation and a safe, effective delivery method remained open questions. Those are the same two obstacles that have constrained melittin's development in oncology.

This review asks how melittin's ability to disrupt cancer cell membranes could be harnessed as a cancer treatment while minimizing damage to healthy cells. The literature has answered with three broad strategies: engineering the peptide sequence, directing the peptide toward tumor-specific receptors, and encapsulating it in a nanocarrier. The argument developed here is that these should be judged not by whether they improve selectivity, which nearly all of them do, but by what they charge for it. Selectivity and potency do trade against one another, though the exchange rate varies enormously. A single-residue substitution can reduce hemolysis thirteenfold while costing threefold in anticancer activity, whereas a carrier may reduce exposure without conferring any tumor specificity at all. The approaches that succeed are those exploiting a mechanistic asymmetry, a route to cell death that cancer cells are vulnerable to and healthy cells are not, rather than those that simply attenuate the peptide.

## Mechanism and the selectivity problem

Any attempt to engineer selectivity has to work with a structure that evolved for indiscriminate membrane destruction, and the difficulty follows from the fact that the structure responsible for potency and the structure responsible for toxicity are largely the same structure. The amphipathic helix Ramirez et al. described partitions into any bilayer presenting the right physical properties, and the charge distribution that drives insertion is the same in a tumor cell membrane and an erythrocyte membrane.<sup>2</sup>

The kinetics compound the problem. Soliman et al. tested melittin against AGS gastric carcinoma cells and the COLO205 and HCT-15 colorectal lines, concentrating on short exposure windows that earlier work had largely overlooked.<sup>4</sup> Killing was dose-dependent, with complete cell death at the highest dose of 20 µg/mL by four hours. Membrane damage, visible as swelling, breakage, and blebbing, began within seconds of exposure, and intracellular material was seen leaving the cells by roughly one minute. The effect was consistent across all three lines, which indicates that melittin ruptures the membrane physically rather than through a pathway a particular tumor type might lack.

That speed cuts both ways. An agent acting within seconds leaves no window for differential uptake to develop, and it gives a targeting moiety very little time to work before off-target lysis has occurred. The mechanism that makes melittin resistant to the efflux pumps and apoptotic blockades defeating conventional chemotherapeutics is the same mechanism that makes it toxic to healthy tissue.

## Strategy one: engineering the peptide sequence

The most direct approach modifies melittin itself. The most conceptually economical example is the enzyme-induced gain of function (EIGF) strategy reported by Li et al., which inverts the usual logic of targeting.<sup>5</sup> Rather than adding a homing signal to an active peptide, the authors disabled the peptide and arranged for the tumor to reactivate it. Phosphorylation at threonine 23 of a melittin analogue (MelA2) disrupted the helical conformation and abolished membrane lysis capacity, and the phosphorylated form, MelA2-P, also lost its cytotoxicity toward normal mammalian cells. Because alkaline phosphatase is more active in cancer cells than in normal cells, dephosphorylation occurs preferentially in the tumor microenvironment, restoring the pore-forming function and killing cancer cells selectively. Selectivity here is a property of where the molecule is switched on rather than of the molecule itself.

Conditional activation sidesteps the structural problem Ramirez identified. Direct substitution within the helix confronts it, and the most informative attempt is also the most quantitatively precise. Lv et al. replaced tryptophan 19, the residue that anchors melittin at the lipid-water interface and which earlier work had implicated in hemolytic activity, with DapAMCA, a diaminopropanoic acid carrying a coumarin-based (AMCA) fluorophore, producing an analogue designated MEL<sup>FL</sup>.<sup>6</sup> The substitution changed the peptide's mode of interaction with the membrane. MEL<sup>FL</sup> still inserted into bilayers but no longer ruptured them efficiently.

The functional consequences separate cleanly. Hemolysis of rabbit erythrocytes required 3 µg/mL of native melittin for half-maximal lysis but 40 µg/mL of MEL<sup>FL</sup>, a thirteenfold reduction in toxicity to healthy cells. Cytotoxicity toward MCF-7 breast cancer cells fell from 10 µg/mL to 28 µg/mL, roughly threefold. MEL<sup>FL</sup> is therefore a weaker agent overall, but it lost far more of its capacity to harm erythrocytes than its capacity to kill tumor cells. Expressed as a selectivity index, the ratio of hemolytic to anticancer concentration, native melittin scores 0.30, below unity, meaning it destroys red cells at doses lower than those required to kill cancer. MEL<sup>FL</sup> scores 1.44, inverting that order, and the Hepa 1-6 liver line showed the same reversal, 0.47 to 1.76.

The mechanistic basis of the reversal is the paper's most useful finding, and it anticipates an argument developed later in this review. Flow cytometry showed that MEL<sup>FL</sup> induced apoptosis in both cancer lines at rates comparable to native melittin, while apoptosis in normal endothelial cells fell from roughly 28 percent to 10 percent. Time-lapse imaging showed the same divergence, with cancer cells swelling and blistering under MEL<sup>FL</sup> while endothelial cells remained morphologically unchanged even at three times the native melittin dose. The analogue had lost lysis and kept the capacity to kill from within. An erythrocyte, lacking nucleus and mitochondria, can be killed only by rupture, whereas a nucleated cancer cell can be killed by rupture or by apoptosis. A peptide that forfeits the first route and keeps the second spares the cell type that was only ever vulnerable to the first.

This result should temper any assumption that selectivity and potency trade one for one. They trade, but the exchange rate is a variable rather than a constant, and here it ran strongly in the therapeutic direction.

A third sequence modification pursues a different goal. Hamdi et al. inserted a WXXW motif into a flexible region of melittin, producing a peptide designated M3 that retains membrane activity while binding doxorubicin reversibly.<sup>7</sup> The rationale exploits melittin's distinctive advantage: because the peptide breaches the membrane physically, it bypasses the efflux pumps that expel conventional cytotoxic drugs, and it can carry a partner agent past that barrier. The authors characterized the construct with liposome leakage assays, fluorescence quenching, microscale thermophoresis, molecular dynamics, calcein AM efflux assays, and cytotoxicity studies in several cancer cell lines, with simulations pointing to the WXXW motif as the initial binding site. The peptide is made to do two jobs at once, breaching the membrane and delivering a payload to cells that have already defeated first-line chemotherapy. Whether this improves selectivity is a separate question, since nothing in the design restricts M3 to tumor cells.

## Strategy two: receptor targeting and combination therapy

The second strategy departs most sharply from the premise that melittin's value lies in lysis. Duffy et al. tested honeybee venom and melittin against HER2-enriched and triple-negative breast cancer and found that both reduced ligand-induced phosphorylation of EGFR and HER2 at the cell membrane, blocking the activation step and altering downstream signaling.<sup>8</sup> Mutational work identified the positively charged C-terminal region as the segment mediating the membrane interaction behind this effect. Melittin was selectively cytotoxic toward EGFR- and HER2-overexpressing lines, and adding an RGD motif improved targeting of malignant cells while keeping toxicity to normal cells low. Melittin appears to have a second mechanism.

Zhong et al. supplied biophysical confirmation that this second mechanism is real rather than incidental.<sup>9</sup> Using an isothermal shift assay together with cellular thermal shift assay, bio-layer interferometry, and AlphaFold3 structural modeling, the authors showed that melittin increased EGFR thermal stability, the signature of direct target engagement, with interferometry giving a dissociation constant of 18.6 nM. Proteomic and phosphoproteomic analysis indicated that binding suppressed EGFR phosphorylation and weakened downstream ERK and STAT3 signaling, inducing autophagy-dependent apoptosis. An EGFR-knockdown PC9 line confirmed the dependency, antitumor effects being substantially weakened when the target was removed. The authors also report a PC9 xenograft model and minimal toxicity toward normal bronchial epithelial cells, and they position melittin as a peptide-based EGFR inhibitor.

The reframing has immediate therapeutic consequences. Ibrahim et al. combined melittin with erlotinib, an established EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor, in A549 NSCLC cells.<sup>10</sup> The combination produced synergistic reductions in viability and migration exceeding either agent alone, alongside enhanced apoptosis; melittin at 50 µM had no discernible effect on migration by itself. In silico docking predicted binding to JAK2 and JAK3, with downstream inactivation of STAT3, as additional contributors. Two independent groups converging on STAT3 signaling is suggestive, though Ibrahim's mechanistic claims rest on computational docking and await experimental confirmation.

Synergy is itself a selectivity strategy, and an underused one. If melittin and erlotinib potentiate each other, each can be given at a lower dose, and melittin's dose-limiting hemolysis is a function of concentration. Lowering the required dose accomplishes indirectly what sequence engineering and encapsulation attempt directly, without the potency penalty those approaches impose.

## Strategy three: nanocarrier delivery

The third strategy leaves the peptide's chemistry alone and addresses where it goes. If melittin cannot easily be made selective, it can at least be kept away from healthy tissue until it arrives at the tumor. Soman et al. provided the foundational demonstration, loading melittin into the outer lipid monolayer of molecularly targeted perfluorocarbon nanoparticles and delivering it to tumor cells in mice.<sup>11</sup> Rather than being endocytosed whole, the particles engaged target cells through hemifusion, transferring melittin directly into the cell membrane. Hemolysis of red blood cells was markedly reduced relative to free peptide, melittin accumulated in murine tumors, and tumor growth fell substantially with no apparent signs of toxicity; precancerous dysplastic lesions regressed. The mechanistic detail most relevant here is easy to overlook: the authors report that hemifusion did not disrupt the target cell surface membrane yet still triggered apoptosis. The nanoparticle preserved a non-lytic route to cell death, which is the same asymmetry that explains the sequence-engineering results above.

Subsequent work clarified that protection and targeting are separable properties, and that achieving the first does not deliver the second. Gonzalez-Horta et al. addressed melittin's antiselectivity, its tendency to lyse erythrocytes as readily as bacteria, by formulating a stable, high-payload poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) nanoparticle carrying melittin in its tetrameric form.<sup>12</sup> The formulation succeeded as chemistry. Tetrameric melittin bound PLGA with 97 percent encapsulation efficiency and retained its lytic activity, inducing leakage from two different liposome compositions. It was not, however, selective. A carrier that shields the peptide in the bloodstream still releases it wherever it ends up.

A third approach addresses durability rather than targeting. Lv et al. substituted the natural L-amino acids of melittin with their D-enantiomers and formulated the result as polymeric nanoparticles.<sup>13</sup> The D-peptide resists enzymatic degradation and provokes less of an adaptive immune response, and the polymer encapsulation allowed higher peptide doses to be given in mice, with improved tumor suppression reported in triple-negative breast cancer models. The gain here is in the therapeutic window rather than in discrimination between cell types. Nothing about the D-form makes the peptide prefer a tumor cell, so a longer-circulating melittin that is not more selective widens the window for off-target hemolysis at the same time as it widens the window for treatment, and the net benefit depends on the carrier keeping the peptide away from erythrocytes.

## Synthesis and limitations

The three strategies can now be compared on the terms this review proposed, which is not whether they improve selectivity but at what rate. Sequence engineering produced the most favorable exchange in the literature surveyed, a thirteenfold reduction in hemolysis against a threefold reduction in anticancer potency, enough to invert the selectivity index from 0.30 to 1.44.<sup>6</sup> Conditional activation avoids the exchange by withholding activity until a tumor-associated enzyme restores it.<sup>5</sup> Receptor-directed approaches also avoid it, though only by abandoning lysis as the primary mechanism.<sup>8,9</sup> Nanocarriers reduce exposure of circulating cells and raise the tolerated dose, but protection alone confers no selectivity, and a carrier that preserves lytic activity preserves it indiscriminately.<sup>11,12,13</sup>

A single principle links the most successful results across all three strategies. MEL<sup>FL</sup> gained selectivity because it lost membrane rupture while retaining apoptosis induction, and erythrocytes, having no nucleus or mitochondria, are vulnerable only to rupture.<sup>6</sup> Soman's nanoparticles killed by hemifusion-triggered apoptosis without disrupting the target cell surface.<sup>11</sup> Duffy and Zhong describe a third non-lytic mechanism, interference with EGFR and HER2 signaling, but the logic is the same in each case: all three exploit a vulnerability that proliferating tumor cells possess and healthy cells either lack or depend on less.<sup>8,9</sup> Melittin's lytic activity, long treated as the therapeutic asset to be aimed more carefully, increasingly looks like the liability to be engineered away while something else is preserved.

Several limitations constrain how far this evidence can be pushed. The entire literature reviewed here is preclinical, confined to cell culture and murine models, with no human trials establishing a therapeutic window in patients. Selectivity demonstrated in vitro is a weak proxy for selectivity in a perfused organism, where melittin meets serum proteins, erythrocytes, and endothelium before reaching any tumor. Hemolysis remains the dose-limiting toxicity throughout, and no approach has eliminated it. In vivo work does exist and is not uniformly old, with murine studies from Soman in 2009, Lv in 2021, and Zhong in 2026, yet none of it has produced a clinical trial.<sup>11,13,9</sup> More than fifteen years separate the first targeted-nanocarrier demonstration from the present without translation, a gap that is itself informative about the difficulty of the problem.

The strongest selectivity result carries its own caveats. Lv's data derive from two cancer lines, MCF-7 and Hepa 1-6, with no animal work, pharmacokinetics, or in vivo toxicity profile, so whether the reversal generalizes is unknown.<sup>6</sup> The structural account of why MEL<sup>FL</sup> loses lytic efficiency rests largely on molecular dynamics simulation, and the peptide's oligomeric state was not measured experimentally. The observation that MEL<sup>FL</sup> accumulates in the nucleus and nucleolus is intriguing but correlational, since localization was demonstrated and causation was not. Mechanistic claims across the field vary similarly in strength, from the direct biophysical target engagement Zhong demonstrates by thermal shift and interferometry to the computational docking predictions Ibrahim reports.

The most promising direction is a combination none of these studies has attempted. Li's enzyme-induced activation makes melittin inert until a tumor-associated enzyme switches it on, and Duffy and Zhong establish that melittin interferes with EGFR independently of lysis. A construct that is both conditionally activated and receptor-directed would impose two independent requirements for cytotoxicity rather than one, and by operating below the lytic threshold it might avoid the potency penalty that has limited every selectivity strategy reviewed here. Whether these mechanisms prove additive or interfering is the obvious next experiment, and it has not been run.
